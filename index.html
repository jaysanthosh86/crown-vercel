The problem · 03 / 06
Diabetes care is already a lot to remember. The pen should help the routine feel clearer, not heavier.

Our honest starting point: this concept focuses on a visible design gap—dose timing, site rotation and follow-up context can be spread across memory, a pen, notes and a phone. We have not been given a personal founder story or collected research results, so this page does not invent either. The next step is to verify the problem with people who use insulin and diabetes-care professionals.

01

“Did I take it already?”

A missing or unclear record can create uncertainty. CROWN aims to log confirmed events, show when a record is uncertain and avoid telling someone to repeat a dose.

02

Site rotation is easy to forget

A simple recent-site history may help a patient follow the rotation plan agreed with their care team. Automatic abdomen/thigh recognition is a research question—not a current capability claim.

03

Appointments give only a snapshot

With consent, a time-stamped log may make it easier to discuss patterns, missed records and practical barriers during a review.

04

Connectivity is not always reliable

Essential confirmation should happen on the device. Phone syncing improves visibility later; it should not be a safety dependency.

Global presence & India fit

Built for the realities of care here.

India has a large diabetes burden, but a large population alone does not prove a market. CROWN should begin with a defined group: adults on daily insulin who use pen devices, have recurring dose-record or site-rotation friction, and are supported by a diabetes clinic or educator.

01 / REGULATION

Approval is not assumed

No CDSCO approval is claimed for CROWN. The intended use and risk class must be reviewed by an Indian medical-device regulatory specialist before trials or sale.

02 / AFFORDABILITY

Price is a design constraint

Test willingness to pay separately in urban clinics and lower-resource settings. Explore a one-time hardware price and a free core patient app before adding any paid tier.

03 / DISTRIBUTION

Different settings, different needs

Urban pilots may be easier to support first. Rural use needs battery life, offline behaviour, repairability, local-language onboarding and low data dependence.

04 / ECOSYSTEM

Local build, careful partners

Explore Make in India-aligned prototyping, Startup India incubation and BIRAC routes where the call and eligibility fit. These programmes are not endorsements or guaranteed funding.

Feasibility & money

Prototype the riskiest assumptions first.

A student-scale proof of concept is feasible with off-the-shelf electronics and a 3D-printed housing. A safe, accurate, compatible, manufacturable medical device is a much larger project requiring testing, quality systems and regulatory work.

Materials & equipment

Use a small BLE dev board, IMU / hall or optical sensors for experiments, a low-power LED, vibration motor, rechargeable cell with protection, custom PCB later, and a 3D-printed enclosure with replaceable adapter collars.

CAD + 3D printerSoldering stationMultimeter / bench supplyPhone test devicesForce / fit fixturesTest logs + version control

Prototype difficulty: medium–high

Electronic sensing and a demonstration app are achievable. Reliable dose-event capture, safe fit across pen brands, cleaning, battery life, sensor accuracy, privacy and a medical-device quality process are the hard parts.

Price hypothesis to test

Start by testing a one-time accessory price around ₹1,999–₹2,999 with a basic app included. This is a willingness-to-pay experiment—not a launch price, validated cost target or promise. Do not set a price until BOM and user interviews are complete.

Why it's worth exploring

  • It targets a concrete daily routine rather than a broad “digital health” pitch.
  • Prototype electronics and housings can be tested incrementally.
  • A shared record may improve the quality of patient–clinician conversations.
  • Modular hardware may avoid replacing an entire pen if compatibility proves possible.

What could stop it

  • Pen-brand differences make one “universal” fit unrealistic.
  • False dose logs could create dangerous confusion.
  • Patients may not pay for a separate accessory.
  • Regulatory, cybersecurity, battery and reliability work may cost more than the hardware.

One-minute explanation

“Imagine your insulin pen had a small smart cap. It could remind you about the routine, help you record when you used the pen, and keep a simple history of the injection sites you confirm. Later, with your permission, the information could appear in an app so you and your doctor can talk about your routine. It would not choose your insulin dose, and it would still need lots of testing before anyone should rely on it.”

The full brief

Answers to the questions investors will ask.

Use these as working answers for a presentation. Update assumptions as interviews, engineering tests and regulatory review produce evidence.

Working draft · concept stage
What is the product, its colour, logo and team identity?

Name: CROWN (working product name). Definition: a proposed modular smart insulin-pen attachment that adds reminders, dose-event logging and user-guided injection-site history with optional app connectivity. Visual identity: matte white shell, deep teal body, signal-blue highlights and a small crown mark. Team identity: “CROWN LAB” is a placeholder lock-up; replace it with the actual team name and member names before presenting. Check trademark availability before using the name commercially.

How would it be used, made and how complex is it?

The intended flow is attach to a tested compatible pen, pair with the app, set a clinician-provided schedule, confirm the site and review the event log. Manufacturing would involve a custom PCB, low-power BLE microcontroller, event sensors, battery protection, LED / haptic output, and a cleanable enclosure with an adapter collar. A 3D-printed demonstrator is medium complexity; reliable sensing, safe compatibility, regulatory and production readiness are high complexity.

Who suffers, and why this problem over others?

The initial group is adults who use insulin pens daily and have difficulty keeping dose records or site rotation organised, especially where care teams would benefit from better between-visit context. The honest rationale is that the routine is frequent and information is split across memory, device and app—not that a founder has lived experience we cannot substantiate. Interviews must confirm how severe and common the problem is, and whether this specific solution is wanted.

Does it already exist? Can CROWN beat the competitors?

Connected products already exist, including Medtronic InPen, NovoPen 6 / Echo Plus, Lilly's Tempo ecosystem and Bigfoot Unity. CROWN should compete on a narrower hypothesis: a modular attachment, quiet on-device guidance, user-confirmed site history and a lower-complexity experience for a specific Indian user segment. It cannot claim superiority until side-by-side testing, interoperability work, pricing research and clinical usability studies support that claim. A major incumbent can outspend a small team on regulatory and distribution.

Which countries use related products, and what do they cost?

Connected insulin systems are sold in selected markets, including the United States and parts of Europe and Australia; country availability differs by product and compatible insulin system. Novo Nordisk lists supported app / device markets on its official NovoPen page. Manufacturer pricing is often affected by prescription, insurance and reimbursement, and a consistent current retail price comparison is not available from public pages. A 2019 review reported historical examples such as Timesulin around £12 and Dukada Trio around €40; these must not be presented as current prices. Obtain current manufacturer and distributor quotes country by country.

Is CROWN approved or in use in India?

No. CROWN is an unvalidated concept shown here for discussion; no CDSCO approval or live medical-device status is claimed. India's Medical Devices Rules, 2017 establish risk-based requirements, and CDSCO has stated that devices require the relevant licensing pathway for manufacture, import, sale and distribution (with specific provisions and exceptions in the rules). Ask a regulatory professional to determine intended-use classification, test requirements, quality obligations and the correct authority before any human pilot or commercial activity.

What is the Indian gap, price target and funding path?

The gap to test is whether a simpler, locally supported accessory can serve a specific pen-using segment that existing ecosystems don't serve affordably or conveniently. The ₹1,999–₹2,999 amount on this page is only a willingness-to-pay hypothesis. Potential routes to investigate include local prototyping, eligible Startup India incubation / seed-funding calls and BIRAC programmes where the current call, entity and project are eligible. Make in India is an alignment opportunity, not a certification. Do not claim Ayushman Bharat reimbursement or programme support without a confirmed pathway.

What are the main risks and how would we respond?
  • False dose records: show uncertainty, never recommend repeat dosing, validate with ground truth.
  • Compatibility: start with one pen model and test fit and function before adding adapters.
  • Price-sensitive buyers: validate willingness to pay before tooling; explore partnerships and low-cost service.
  • Brand loyalty: work with patient groups, clinicians and device manufacturers rather than claim universal fit.
  • Regulatory delay: engage a device specialist early and budget for testing, quality systems and evidence.
  • Privacy / cyber risk: use data minimisation, encryption, consent, retention controls and a threat model.
What is the patent plan and long-term future?

First do prior-art and freedom-to-operate searches, then document novelty and ownership. Consult a patent professional before public disclosure and decide whether a provisional patent, design registration, software copyright or trade-secret approach is appropriate. A future roadmap could add tested adapters for more pen families, Indian-language accessibility, caregiver sharing, validated CGM integrations, clinic reporting, replaceable batteries or lower-power electronics—but only after user demand and safety have been demonstrated.
